Where does the sound go?

This app is a screening sketch. It estimates how machine sound spreads in an ideal open space. It does not tell you whether someone in town will hear it.

1. Start with the machine's sound power

The number on a machine badge is LwA: how much A-weighted sound power the machine emits. It's a property of the source. The starter values are guesses, not measurements from Charles Hull or Talison.

A receiver's number is LpA: the estimated sound-pressure level at that location. These are different quantities. A familiar “60 dB conversation” is a pressure-level comparison, not a machine power input.

2. Spread that power over a growing sphere

In an ideal free field, doubling the distance reduces the level by about 6 dB. The app uses distance in metres—not kilometres in a formula expecting metres.

10 m69 dB(A)100 m49 dB(A)1 km29 dB(A)

Example: one 100 dB(A) LwA source, geometric spreading only.

Show the equation

LpA = LwA − 20 log10(distance in metres) − 10 log10(4π)

Power is referenced to 1 picowatt; pressure is referenced to 20 micropascals. The conversion assumes conventional free-field reference conditions. Distances under 1 m use a numerical clamp and are not a near-field prediction.

3. Add energy, not the dB numbers

Two independent machines each producing 60 dB(A) at a receiver combine to about 63 dB(A), not 120. Contours connect points with the same combined predicted level. They show independent-machine energy addition, not phase cancellation.

So why did a number appear near Perth?

The old model extrapolated geometric spreading indefinitely. A 120 dB(A) LwA source still gives roughly 3 dB(A) at 200 km in that idealised equation. That is not evidence the machine can be heard there.

The equation omits atmospheric absorption, ground effects, terrain, barriers and weather-related refraction. It also omits background noise, which can mask a much smaller machine contribution. We have not invented extra attenuation to make the picture look more believable.

At your request, receiver numbers are now hidden when the nearest machine is more than 10 km away. A contour extending beyond 10 km from every nearby source is also not displayed, and its level is marked failed. This is an explicit screening scope, not a claim that sound stops at 10 km. Even inside it, this model is unvalidated. When a nearby receiver's total includes more distant source paths, the app warns you rather than silently dropping them.

Can sound be negative?

Decibels can be negative; sound energy cannot. Zero dB is a reference pressure, not zero sound. −20 dB corresponds to 2 micropascals, below the 20-micropascal reference.

A negative calculated value is not automatically a coding error. But a tiny number produced by extrapolating this simplified model over a huge distance is not a credible environmental assessment. The app keeps legitimate negative levels inside its screening scope; it does not clamp them to a misleading 0 dB.

What the app leaves out

Actual equipment sound-power evidence and frequency spectra, source/receiver heights and directionality, reflective ground, pits and hills, buildings/barriers, absorption, wind and temperature profiles, operating cycles, background measurements and uncertainty. Cloud cover is recorded only; it has no numerical correction.

For due diligence, agree the assessment criteria, gather appropriate data, use and validate a suitable propagation method, compare with measurements and document mitigation and operating controls. A neat contour is not a compliance or inaudibility certificate.

Using the map and saved scenarios

Machine icons show an illustrative equipment symbol and a number. Hover or focus an icon to see its name and sound power; click it to open the evidence note. Receiver badges show predicted pressure, or a clear pending/error/out-of-scope state. Colours show level, not legal compliance.

Default contours match the five illustrative levels in the top-right sound guide: 30, 40, 60, 80 and 100 dB(A). Each level calculates independently. Failed levels show their own reason; the other levels remain visible. High contrast adds white halos without changing the physics.

Save and Open keep named scenarios on the computer running the Python backend, including source notes, camera and contour settings. The saved URL reopens the last saved version on reload. A browser draft can recover unsaved edits after you choose Restore. It does not update named saves automatically and is not a backup. Back up the backend's local-data/scenarios folder; this is not a cloud synchronisation service.
